3 The concept

Formal academic and professional connectors

These connectors mark contrast, concession and reasoning in a more formal register than everyday "but", "so" or "because" — common in reports, academic writing and formal presentations.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Nonetheless (formal "however"): usually starts or joins a new sentence. "Sales fell; nonetheless, profit rose."
✅ Notwithstanding + noun phrase (formal "despite"): "Notwithstanding the setbacks, the launch proceeded."
✅ Whereas + clause (formal "while", for contrast); In light of + noun phrase ("given"); Insofar as + clause ("to the extent that").
🎯 The tricky part: "notwithstanding" takes a noun phrase, not a full clause, and can even follow the noun phrase it modifies in very formal style: "the risks notwithstanding" or "notwithstanding the risks" — both are correct.

4 Reading — Why Effort Shrinks in Teams: The Ringelmann Effect

When Max Ringelmann conducted his now-famous rope-pulling experiments in 1913, he had no idea he was about to name an effect that would apply just as neatly to modern meetings as to farm labourers pulling on a rope. Individuals, tested alone, pulled with a certain measurable force, whereas that same effort, once spread across a group, was found to shrink steadily as more people joined in. Had each participant been pulling entirely alone, the total recorded force would have been dramatically higher.

Notwithstanding a century of further research, the exact psychological mechanism remains only partly understood, though most explanations point to reduced accountability: in a large group, diffused responsibility makes any single person's contribution feel less consequential. Rarely does anyone in a large team consciously decide to slack off; coasting tends to happen quietly, below the level of conscious awareness. Having studied dozens of workplace teams since, researchers now describe this social loafing as one of the most replicated findings in group psychology.

In light of this evidence, some companies have begun deliberately shrinking team sizes for tasks that reward individual accountability, while keeping larger teams, insofar as the work genuinely benefits from parallel effort, for tasks that don't. The effect is not universal — nonetheless, it appears with remarkable consistency across cultures, industries and even species, underscoring just how deeply ingrained the pattern seems to be. A manager, trained to notice the pattern, can design around it; one, left unaware, will simply keep adding people to a struggling project and wonder why productivity per head keeps falling.
