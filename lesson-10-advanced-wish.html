Advanced Wish / If Only

Wish + would have, and layered regret: "I wish I'd known, because if I had, I wouldn't have..."

1 Warm-up

You already know "I wish I had done" for past regret. Today we add "wish + would have" for annoyance at someone else's past action, and practise chaining regret across two linked clauses.

1. "I wish he would have told me sooner" expresses…

2. "If only I had checked the schedule, I wouldn't have missed the flight" chains together…

3. "I wish I'd known, because if I had, I wouldn't have signed" is an example of…

💡 "Wish + would have" almost always signals irritation about someone else's (in)action — it's rarely neutral, and it's rarely used about yourself.

3 The concept

From simple regret to layered regret

"Wish + past perfect" is regret about your own past. "Wish + would" is frustration about someone else's repeated behaviour. And you can chain a wish onto a third conditional to spell out exactly what the consequence would have been.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Wish/If only + subject + had + past participle — regret about your own past.
✅ Wish + subject + would(n't) + base verb — frustration at someone else's action/behaviour (never used about yourself).
✅ Wish + past perfect, + because if + past perfect, + would(n't) have + past participle — layering a wish onto its exact consequence.
🎯 The tricky part: "wish + would" is almost never used about yourself, and never for something you have no control over changing about the present moment.

❌ "I wish I would stop being late." — sounds wrong; use "I wish I wouldn't be late" (rare) or, far more naturally, "I wish I weren't always late."
✅ "I wish he would stop being late." — perfectly natural, aimed at someone else's behaviour.

For layered regret, keep the logic consistent: the "if" clause should restate the exact same past action as the wish, so the consequence clause follows logically.

4 Reading — Defending the Decision You Already Made

When a decision turns out badly, the mind rarely settles for simple regret. Instead it experiences dissonance — the discomfort of holding two contradictory beliefs at once: "I am a careful decision-maker" and "I just approved a project that failed badly." "I wish I had asked more questions before signing off," the operations director admitted afterward. "Because if I had, we might never have overcommitted to that vendor in the first place."

Rather than sit with that discomfort, people typically rationalise the original choice instead of abandoning it — reinterpreting the evidence, minimising the failure, or quietly rewriting their own memory of how confident they actually felt at the time. "I wish the board would stop pretending the numbers looked fine from the start," one finance manager complained; "they didn't, and everyone in that room knew it."

The pattern intensifies with sunk cost: the more money and reputation already invested, the harder people work to justify continuing rather than admit the original call was wrong. "If only we had cancelled the contract after the first missed deadline," the director said quietly, "we would have saved ourselves eighteen months of this." Colleagues nodded; nobody disagreed, but nobody had said so at the time either.

By the final review, the regret had layered into something more complicated than simple hindsight. "I wish I hadn't reassured the board that everything was under control," the director reflected, "because if I hadn't, someone might have challenged the decision months earlier, before it became impossible to unwind." Learning to reframe a bad decision honestly — rather than defending it indefinitely — turned out to be the harder, and more valuable, lesson.
