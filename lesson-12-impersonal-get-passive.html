Impersonal Passive & Get-Passive Together

"It is said that...", "he is believed to have...", and when "get" beats "be" in the passive.

1 Warm-up

The impersonal passive ("it is said that...", "he is believed to have...") lets us report opinions or rumours without naming who holds them. The get-passive ("got fired", "got promoted") is a more informal alternative to "be", often used for sudden or unwelcome changes.

1. "It is believed that the merger will go ahead" avoids naming…

2. "He is thought to have resigned voluntarily" is a more formal way of saying…

3. "She got promoted last month" sounds more…

💡 Impersonal passive = formal, source hidden on purpose. Get-passive = informal, often for changes of state, especially unwelcome or sudden ones.

3 The concept

Hiding the source vs. choosing an informal tone

The impersonal passive reports beliefs, rumours or claims without naming a source — useful in news and formal writing. The get-passive is an informal alternative to "be + p.p.", often implying something sudden, unexpected, or affecting the subject personally.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Impersonal passive: It is + p.p. + that... or subject + is/are + p.p. + to-infinitive — reports a claim, belief or rumour with no named source.
✅ Get-passive: get/got + past participle — an informal alternative to "be", often for sudden, unwelcome or dynamic changes.
🎯 The tricky part: get-passive doesn't work with every verb, and it sounds distinctly informal — never use it in a formal report or academic writing.

✅ "She got promoted." — natural, informal, common in spoken English.
✅ "She was promoted." — the neutral, formal equivalent, used in reports and official announcements.
❌ "It is got said that..." — get-passive never combines with impersonal "it" constructions; those stay strictly with "be".

Rule of thumb: reach for "get" in casual speech about personal change, and "be" (plain or impersonal) everywhere formal.

4 Reading — Confirmation Bias in Hiring Decisions

It is widely believed that hiring decisions are the product of careful, objective comparison, but researchers who study interviews describe something far less rational. An interviewer typically forms a strong impression of a candidate within the first few minutes, often unconsciously, and everything that follows tends to be read through that initial lens.

This pattern is known as confirmation bias, and once it takes hold, the rest of the interview quietly becomes a search for evidence supporting the first impression rather than a genuine test of it. A candidate who built early rapport with the interviewer is said to handle pressure well and think on their feet, even when the actual answers are fairly ordinary. A candidate who struck the interviewer badly, by contrast, is believed to have performed poorly overall, regardless of how strong the individual answers actually were. Small, ambiguous moments in the conversation — a pause, a nervous laugh — get interpreted in whichever direction the first impression has already skewed the interviewer's judgement.

A related phenomenon, the halo effect, makes the bias even stronger: an interviewer who is impressed by one strong quality — confidence, a firm handshake, a shared hobby — tends to assume every other quality is equally impressive. As a result, some genuinely strong candidates get overlooked simply because they made a flat first impression, while a confident but underqualified candidate sometimes gets hired instead. It is now recommended by most hiring specialists that interviewers use structured scoring sheets and delay their overall judgement until every candidate's credentials have been reviewed side by side, precisely to stop the first few minutes from overshadowing everything that comes after.
