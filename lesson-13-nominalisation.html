Nominalisation

Turning verbs and adjectives into nouns for a more formal, impersonal, report-like style.

1 Warm-up

Nominalisation means turning a verb or adjective into a noun — "they decided" becomes "the decision was made", "implement" becomes "implementation". It's one of the biggest markers of formal, academic and business writing.

1. "They decided to expand the team" nominalised becomes…

2. Nominalisation tends to make writing sound more…

3. The noun form of "implement" (a verb) is…

💡 If you can replace a verb or adjective with an -ion/-ment/-ness/-ity noun and add a supporting verb like "make", "reach" or "undergo", you're nominalising.

3 The concept

Verbs and adjectives packed into nouns

Formal reports often turn actions and qualities into noun phrases, then attach a light supporting verb (make, reach, undergo, conduct, carry out). This shifts focus from who did what to the process or result itself.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Verb → noun: -ion, -ment, -al, -ure (decide→decision, implement→implementation, arrive→arrival, fail→failure).
✅ Adjective → noun: -ity, -ness, -ance/-ence (significant→significance, aware→awareness, efficient→efficiency).
🎯 The tricky part: nominalisation isn't just a vocabulary swap — it also changes the sentence's grammar, usually shifting from an active subject-verb-object to a passive or "there was/is a..." structure with a light verb.

✅ "They implemented the policy quickly." → "The implementation of the policy was swift." (subject shifts from "they" to the process itself)
✅ Overusing nominalisation makes writing feel dense and impersonal — perfect for a formal report, wrong for a friendly email.

Use it deliberately: when you want to sound objective and process-focused, not when you're just trying to sound smart.

4 Reading — The Peter Principle: Promoted Beyond Competence

In many organisations, an employee's promotion is treated as straightforward proof of merit: strong performance in one role is assumed to guarantee success in the next. The Peter Principle, first proposed decades ago, challenges that assumption directly. Following extended observation of hierarchical companies, the theory concludes that employees tend to rise until they reach a position in which they are no longer competent, and then remain there indefinitely, since further advancement effectively stops.

The logic behind this pattern is simple, if uncomfortable. Selection for a new role is based almost entirely on proficiency in the current one, even though the two positions often demand entirely different skills. A brilliant technician may show excellent attention to detail, qualities that lead naturally to a move into management — a role that instead requires delegation, negotiation and interpersonal judgement, skills the technician was never actually tested on. The assessment behind that move, in other words, measured entirely the wrong thing.

Once installed in a role beyond their competence, the newly promoted employee typically produces weaker results than before, yet organisations rarely reverse the decision. A formal demotion is administratively awkward and can look like a public admission of failure, so the underperforming manager is usually left exactly where they are, contributing to a slow stagnation across the whole workforce, as talented staff below them wait indefinitely for a vacancy that never opens. The incompetence the theory describes, then, is less an individual failing than a structural one, built into how promotions are decided in the first place.
