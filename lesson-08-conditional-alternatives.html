Conditional Alternatives to If

Provided that, as long as, unless, suppose, on condition that — precision beyond a plain "if".

1 Warm-up

"If" is neutral and general. Other conditional linkers add nuance — a stricter requirement, an exception, a negotiated term. Recognising them is essential for contracts, negotiations and formal proposals.

1. "We'll sign the deal, provided that the price stays fixed" adds a…

2. "Unless you object, we'll proceed as planned" means we'll proceed…

3. "Suppose the client rejects the offer — what's our next move?" is used to…

💡 "Unless" secretly means "if...not" — it's the one that trips people up most, because the negative is built into the word itself.
2 Quick check — before we explain anything

Four quick questions. This isn't graded harshly — it just shows us where to focus.

1. You can take the afternoon off, ___ you finish the report first.

2. We won't renew the contract ___ the terms improve.

3. The vendor agreed to the deadline, ___ we paid a deposit upfront.

4. ___ the client calls before Friday, what should I tell them?

3 The concept

Five ways to say "if", each with its own flavour

These linkers all introduce a condition, but they aren't interchangeable — each carries a slightly different shade of meaning, from strict requirement to pure hypothesis.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Provided (that) / As long as + clause — a strict, near-mandatory requirement.
✅ Unless + clause — equivalent to "if...not"; an exception to the main statement.
✅ Suppose / Supposing + clause — a hypothetical raised for discussion; On condition that + clause — a formal negotiated term.
🎯 The tricky part: never double the negative with "unless".

❌ "Unless you don't finish, you can leave." — this accidentally means the opposite of what's intended.
✅ "Unless you finish, you can't leave." — "unless" already carries the "not", so the clause after it should be positive.

Also, "provided that" and "as long as" both take a present tense even for future meaning, exactly like "if": "provided that it arrives on time" (not "will arrive").

4 Reading — Why Losing Feels Worse Than Winning Feels Good

People tend to weigh losses roughly twice as heavily as equivalent gains — a well-documented asymmetry that psychologists call loss aversion. This bias shapes decisions well beyond the laboratory. An employee will keep funding a mediocre project provided that killing it means formally admitting a loss, yet the same employee would walk away from an equally-sized potential gain without a second thought.

The effect depends heavily on the reference point a person starts from. A manager who has already mentally spent a bonus treats losing part of it as a real loss, not merely a smaller gain — and as long as that framing holds, she will take startling risks simply to avoid confirming the loss. Teams often become entrenched in failing strategies for exactly this reason: cutting losses feels worse than it logically should, purely because of how the brain accounts for money already committed.

Sensible risk-taking is still possible, but usually only under certain conditions. Employees say they would try a genuinely risky idea provided that the downside is capped in advance — unless the cap is missing entirely, in which case almost nobody volunteers. "Suppose we removed the safeguard of a spending ceiling altogether," one operations director asked her team; "would anyone still take the same risk?" Nobody said yes. The company now approves experimental projects only on condition that a maximum loss threshold is agreed before work begins.
