Narrative Tenses in Extended Storytelling

Weaving past simple, continuous and perfect fluidly across a whole story.

1 Warm-up

You already know past simple, past continuous and past perfect individually. Today is about weaving all three together across a longer stretch of narrative — the way real stories, reports and anecdotes actually work.

1. In a story, the tense that carries the main sequence of events forward is usually…

2. "It was raining when the meeting started" uses past continuous to describe…

3. "By the time she arrived, everyone had left" uses past perfect because leaving happened…

💡 Think of past simple as the story's spine, past continuous as its scenery, and past perfect as flashbacks — that's the whole lesson in one sentence.

3 The concept

Three tenses, three jobs in one story

Past simple moves the story forward, event by event. Past continuous paints the background it happens against. Past perfect reaches back to something that was already finished before that point in the story. Browse each below, or test yourself with Quick check.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Past Simple: subject + verb-ed / irregular past — moves the plot forward.
✅ Past Continuous: subject + was/were + verb-ing — sets the scene, or gets interrupted.
✅ Past Perfect: subject + had + past participle — a flashback to something already finished.
🎯 The tricky part: past perfect is only needed when the order of two past events would otherwise be ambiguous or worth emphasising — not every earlier action needs it.

✅ "She left the office and went home." — simple sequence, order is obvious from word order alone, no past perfect needed.
✅ "She left the office because she had finished early." — past perfect clarifies that finishing happened before leaving, not as part of the same moment.

Overusing past perfect ("had left, had gone, had arrived...") for a plain sequence makes a story sound clumsy — save it for genuine flashbacks or cause-before-effect.

4 Reading — The Halo Effect in Performance Reviews

Elena had already formed a strong impression of David long before his official review began. Back in spring, he had delivered a dazzling presentation to the board, and the memory of that single afternoon overshadowed nearly everything that came after. While she was flicking through his file the night before the meeting, she realised she could barely recall a single other thing he had done since.

During the review itself, Elena praised David's "consistent excellence" and credited him with initiative he had barely shown that quarter. She glossed over two missed deadlines, because by then that one dazzling afternoon had already convinced her he was reliable. Meanwhile, two desks away, a quieter colleague, Priya, was quietly finishing a meticulous project that would go on to save the department real money — work that barely earned a mention in her own review.

Psychologists have a name for what happened that afternoon: the halo effect, first described nearly a century ago by the psychologist Edward Thorndike, occurs when one striking quality skewed judgement of everything else. David's rating that year came out noticeably inflated, while Priya's looked oddly distorted downward, for no clear reason at all. It was only when an external auditor compared the numbers months later that the mistake finally backfired, forcing the company to rewrite its entire review process.
