Fronting & Information Structure

Moving an object, complement or adverbial to the front of a sentence to control emphasis and cohesion.

1 Warm-up

English word order is normally fixed (subject-verb-object), but writers often move an element to the front to emphasise it or link it smoothly to the previous sentence — a technique called fronting.

1. "Such was the demand that tickets sold out in minutes" emphasises…

2. "A particularly thorny issue is client confidentiality" fronts the complement to…

3. "This report, we cannot publish without legal review" fronts the object mainly to…

💡 Fronting is optional, not required by grammar rules — writers choose it purely for emphasis or to link ideas smoothly across sentences.

3 The concept

Fronting an object, complement, or adverbial

Fronting moves something other than the subject to the front of the sentence, for emphasis or to connect smoothly with what came before. "Such/so" fronting with an inverted clause is a particularly formal variant.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Fronted object: object + subject + verb — "This report, we cannot publish yet."
✅ Fronted complement: complement + be + subject — "A major concern is data security."
✅ Such/So + inversion: Such + be + subject + that... or So + adjective + be + subject + that... — "Such was the demand that we sold out in an hour."
🎯 The tricky part: fronting a complement with "be" often requires subject-verb inversion too, because the complement is now doing the job a subject normally does at the front of the sentence.

✅ "A recurring problem is staff turnover." (complement fronted, "is" stays before the real subject "staff turnover")
✅ "Such was the demand that tickets sold out." — inversion after "such" is fixed and non-negotiable in this pattern.

Fronting an object rarely needs inversion — the subject and verb usually keep their normal order after the fronted object.

4 Reading — Why We Love What We Build Ourselves

Such was the appeal of self-assembled furniture that one Swedish retailer built an entire empire on it. Researchers studying the phenomenon found something curious: people who assemble a piece of furniture themselves consistently rate it as more valuable than an identical, pre-built version — even when the finished product looks objectively worse. A particularly striking finding was that this effect held even for laborious tasks that most participants openly disliked while doing them.

This attachment, psychologists suspect, has little to do with the object itself and everything to do with effort. So strong is the pull of one's own labour that people will overvalue a wobbly, imperfect shelf simply because they built it themselves. Also documented is a matching effect in cooking, gardening and even spreadsheets: people rate a recipe, a garden, or a file as better once they have invested real effort building it.

This flawed final product, participants rarely blame; instead, they grow quietly sentimental about the very screws and brackets that gave them so much trouble. A key implication for flat-pack retailers, and for anyone designing a product, is that a little friction, a chance to tinker — deliberately included — can build stronger loyalty than a flawless, ready-made version ever could. Marketers now sometimes call this effort justification: we justify our effort, after the fact, by inflating the value of what that effort produced.
