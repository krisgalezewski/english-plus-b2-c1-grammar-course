Future in the Past + Future Perfect Continuous

Talking about a future that never arrived, and a future duration measured from further ahead.

1 Warm-up

"Future in the past" describes a plan or prediction made at some point in the past, about what would happen next — often one that didn't actually happen. Future perfect continuous, meanwhile, looks forward to how long something will have been going on.

1. "She was going to call you, but she forgot" describes a plan that…

2. "By next June, I will have been working here for ten years" focuses on…

3. "He said he would call later" is future-in-the-past because the future was viewed…

💡 Future-in-the-past is always about a future seen from an earlier vantage point — often one that got disrupted.

3 The concept

A future seen from the past, and a future measured backward

"Was/were going to" and "would" describe a future as it looked from an earlier point — often one that was cancelled or changed. Future perfect continuous measures how long something will have continued by a future point.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Future in the past: was/were going to or would + base verb — a future viewed from an earlier moment.
✅ Future perfect continuous: will have been + verb-ing — duration up to a future point.
🎯 The tricky part: "future in the past" almost always implies the plan changed, was cancelled, or simply never came to pass — that's usually the whole point of using it rather than a plain past tense.

✅ "We were going to move to Berlin, but the job offer fell through." — a cancelled plan, viewed from the past.
✅ Compare with future perfect continuous, which never implies cancellation — it's a confident projection forward, purely about duration: "By June, she will have been living here for five years."

4 Reading — The Sunk Cost Fallacy in Project Management

When Aurora kicked off, the roadmap was going to launch the platform in under six months, and engineers promised migration would be complete well before the holiday season. Everyone was confident; the projected timeline looked, if anything, generous.

Then costs began to spiral. A mid-project audit revealed that the chosen database architecture couldn't handle Aurora's expected traffic, and switching now would mean discarding eight months of work. Leadership had already assured the board that they were not going to reconsider the architecture at this stage — too much had already been sunk into it. Rather than cut losses, the steering committee insisted that by the following fiscal year, Aurora would have been generating more than enough revenue to justify every delay.

That prediction never came true. Researchers who study the sunk cost fallacy — our tendency to keep funnelling resources into a failing effort simply because we've already committed so much — would recognise the pattern instantly: the more the company spent trying to save Aurora, the harder abandoning it became to justify. Analysts now estimate that by the time a genuine replacement finally ships, the company will have been paying for Aurora's upkeep for nearly three years longer than the original plan ever intended — a stark reminder that a plan can quietly outlive the reasons anyone had for trusting it.
