Advanced Relative Clauses

Whereby, whereupon, whole-clause 'which', and prepositions placed before whom/which in formal style.

1 Warm-up

Beyond who/which/that, formal and written English uses a small set of advanced relative structures that let a clause refer to a whole idea, a process, or attach a preposition more elegantly.

1. "A system whereby employees can report issues anonymously" means a system…

2. "She was promoted, which surprised nobody" — what does "which" refer to?

3. In formal writing, "the colleague to whom I reported" is preferred over…

💡 "Whereby" = by which/through which (a process). A comma + "which" referring back to a whole clause is extremely common in professional writing. Preposition-before-relative-pronoun (to whom, on which) is the formal register's version of ending a sentence with a preposition.

3 The concept

Beyond who, which and that

"Whereby" introduces a process or mechanism. "Whereupon" introduces an immediate consequence, very formal and often literary. A comma + "which" can refer to an entire preceding clause, not just a noun. Prepositions placed before whom/which sound more formal than stranding them at the end.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Whereby: noun + whereby + clause = by/through which. "A policy whereby latecomers are fined."
✅ Comma + which: refers back to the whole previous clause, not a single noun. "He resigned suddenly, which shocked everyone."
✅ Preposition + whom/which: to/with/on/for + whom/which — formal, never with "that" or "who" in this position.
🎯 The tricky part: "whereupon" (= and then, immediately) is much rarer and more literary than "whereby" — don't confuse the two, and don't overuse "whereupon" in ordinary business writing.

✅ "A policy whereby employees can request remote work." — describes a mechanism.
✅ "She raised the objection, whereupon the meeting was adjourned." — an immediate, almost dramatic consequence.

Also: never write "to who" or "for which that" — prepositions combine only with whom (people) or which (things), never with "that".

4 Reading — Reading Between the Lines: High-Context vs. Low-Context Teams

Global teams often stumble not over what is said, but over how it is said. Researchers describe a spectrum of communication styles whereby meaning is conveyed either directly, through explicit words, or indirectly, through context, tone and shared assumptions. In a low-context culture, a manager typically states an instruction outright; in a high-context culture, the same message is often left as subtext, wrapped in enough ambiguity that no one need lose face if it is declined. The colleague to whom an indirect request is addressed may need considerable tact simply to work out what is actually being asked.

Managers frequently misjudge these signals, which explains why so many cross-border projects stall within the first few months. A manager's bluntness, entirely normal in a low-context office, can easily be misconstrued as outright hostility elsewhere. In one well-known account, a Japanese colleague raised a gentle, indirect objection, whereupon the meeting was quietly rescheduled without anyone stating the real reason aloud.

The issue on which most cross-cultural trainers agree is that neither style is objectively better — each simply optimises for something different: candour and speed in low-context settings, deference and long-term harmony in high-context ones. Experienced managers develop a kind of bilingual sensitivity, which allows them to read nuance in one setting and be reassuringly direct in another. The manager with whom a new hire feels comfortable enough to ask what they actually mean has usually already solved most of the problem.
