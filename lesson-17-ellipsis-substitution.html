Ellipsis & Substitution

Leaving words out and replacing whole clauses to sound natural, concise and fluent.

1 Warm-up

Fluent English constantly avoids repeating itself. Ellipsis omits words that are obvious from context; substitution replaces a whole idea with a short word like "so", "neither" or "one". Both make speech and writing tighter and more natural.

1. "I think so" is a shorter way of saying…

2. "She finished early, and so did I" means…

3. "Call me if necessary" is a shortened form of…

💡 Substitution swaps a whole clause for a small word (so, neither, one). Ellipsis simply deletes words the listener can easily reconstruct from context.

3 The concept

Ellipsis vs. substitution

Ellipsis deletes repeated words entirely ("if necessary" = "if it is necessary"). Substitution replaces a whole clause or noun with a small placeholder word (so, neither, one, do). Both avoid clumsy repetition.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Ellipsis: if/when/though + (subject + be) + adjective/participle — "Contact HR if unsure." (= if you are unsure)
✅ Substitution with so/not: think/hope/believe/expect/say + so/not — "Will it work?" "I expect so."
✅ So/neither + auxiliary + subject: so did I / neither does she / nor can they — agreeing with a previous statement, positive or negative.
🎯 The tricky part: "so did I" (positive agreement) vs. "neither/nor did I" (negative agreement) invert subject and auxiliary — a very common C1 slip is forgetting the inversion.

❌ "She didn't attend, and I neither."
✅ "She didn't attend, and neither did I."

Also: "one/ones" replaces a countable noun already mentioned, but never an uncountable one — "I'd like some more information" not "some more one".

4 Reading — The Secret Doubts of High Achievers

Even the most outwardly accomplished professionals often carry a private conviction that they do not really deserve their success — a pattern researchers term impostor syndrome. A surgeon who has performed hundreds of meticulous operations may still privately suspect that her results come down to luck rather than skill; a professor may attribute a landmark paper to a generous co-author rather than to his own competence. "Do you ever feel like a total fraud?" one colleague asked a newly promoted director. "Honestly? I think so, most days," she admitted.

Psychologists have found that the feeling rarely fades with more evidence of success; if anything, it can become chronic. One study interviewed dozens of high achievers and found that most had never told a colleague about it — and neither had their closest friends outside work, in many cases. When a mentor once confided her own doubts at a team dinner, a junior colleague admitted she felt exactly the same way. "I thought it was just me," she said, "and apparently so did half the department."

Sufferers often dismiss compliments outright, treating genuine praise as unearned and quietly waiting to be exposed as a fraud. Therapists recommend keeping a written record of achievements, to be reread if necessary whenever self-doubt resurfaces, and seeking outside validation only sparingly. "Should I mention this to my manager?" one client asked. "Only if it genuinely comes up — but if so, be honest about it," the therapist replied. "I hope it helps." "I hope so too," the client said quietly.
