Academic Hedging: Seem To, Appear To, Tend To, Be Likely To

Sounding careful and precise instead of overconfident — a register skill, not a tense.

1 Warm-up

At C1 level, sounding credible often means sounding appropriately cautious. Hedging language softens a claim without weakening its substance — a skill used constantly in reports, research and professional writing.

1. "The data seems to suggest a correlation" is more cautious than…

2. Hedging language is used mainly to…

3. "Younger employees tend to prefer flexible hours" is a claim about…

💡 Hedging isn't weakness — it's precision. It signals exactly how confident you are, rather than pretending to certainty you don't have.

3 The concept

Four ways to hedge a claim

Each hedging phrase softens a claim slightly differently — seem/appear to soften based on evidence, tend to signals a general pattern with exceptions, and be likely to signals probability about the future.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Seem to / Appear to + base verb — a cautious claim grounded in evidence: "The plan seems to be succeeding."
✅ Tend to + base verb — a general pattern, not a universal rule: "Junior staff tend to ask more questions."
✅ Be likely to + base verb — a probability about the future: "Costs are likely to rise next year."
🎯 The tricky part: these aren't interchangeable — each hedges a different kind of claim.

✅ "The results seem to confirm the theory." — evidence-based caution about something already observed.
❌ "The results tend to confirm the theory." — wrong: "tend to" describes a repeated pattern across cases, not a one-off observation.
✅ "Prices are likely to rise." — a forward-looking probability, not an observation about the present.

4 Reading — The Dunning-Kruger Effect and Misplaced Confidence

People with limited competence in a given skill tend to overestimate their own ability, according to research popularised by the psychologists David Dunning and Justin Kruger. Their studies appear to reveal a curious pattern: the least skilled participants in a task consistently rate their own performance as exceptional, while genuinely skilled participants, if anything, rate themselves slightly too low.

Novices seem to lack the very expertise that would let them spot their own mistakes — a kind of double handicap researchers describe as incompetence compounding itself. Someone who barely understands a subject is unlikely to notice the gaps in their own reasoning, since spotting a gap requires the same knowledge that person doesn't have. This paradox may explain why the loudest voice in a meeting is likely to sound far more certain than the facts alone would justify.

The effect appears to fade once people gain genuine experience, at which point confidence usually realigns with actual competence — sometimes dipping briefly below it, a dip researchers nickname the trough of despair, before climbing back toward something closer to accurate calibration. None of this makes confidence untrustworthy in general; it simply means that confidence left unchecked by honest feedback tends to drift away from reality, in either direction, and usually in the most confident direction of all.
