Participle Clauses

Using -ing, -ed and having + past participle clauses to write tighter, more fluent sentences.

1 Warm-up

Participle clauses let you fold a relative or adverbial clause into a shorter, more economical structure — very common in reports, emails and formal writing, where wordiness reads as sloppy.

1. "Having reviewed the proposal, she approved it" replaces which longer structure?

2. "Concerned about the delay, the client called twice" explains the client's reason by using…

3. "The report, written by three departments, took months" uses a participle clause instead of…

💡 Present participle (-ing) = active/ongoing; past participle (-ed/3rd form) = passive/completed; "having + past participle" = completed before the main action.

3 The concept

Three participle forms, three jobs

Present participle clauses (-ing) usually show an active or simultaneous action. Past participle clauses (-ed/irregular) show a passive meaning or a state. "Having + past participle" shows something completed before the main clause's action.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Present participle: verb-ing — active, ongoing, or simultaneous with the main verb. "Feeling exhausted, she left early."
✅ Past participle: verb-ed / 3rd form — passive meaning or a resulting state. "Written in haste, the memo contained several errors."
✅ Having + past participle: having + verb-ed/3rd form — completed clearly before the main action. "Having signed the contract, they shook hands."
🎯 The tricky part: the subject of the participle clause must be the same as the subject of the main clause — otherwise you get a "dangling participle", a classic C1 error.

❌ "Walking into the office, the lights were off." (the lights weren't walking)
✅ "Walking into the office, she noticed the lights were off."

Also remember: participle clauses only work when the two actions/subjects genuinely belong together — don't force one in just to sound formal.

4 Reading — The Number That Anchors Everything

Salary negotiations rarely unfold as rationally as we like to imagine. Having anchored on the first figure mentioned, both sides tend to judge every subsequent number in relation to it, rather than on its own merits. Psychologists call this the anchoring effect: once a number enters the room, it quietly reshapes what counts as reasonable, disproportionately favouring whoever names it first. A candidate, concerned about seeming unreasonable, will often accept a lowball offer far sooner than the market would justify, while a recruiter gains real leverage simply by speaking first.

Framed as a routine opening figure, the initial number rarely feels like a tactic at all — yet benchmark studies show that candidates who receive a high opening figure end up with significantly higher final salaries than those who don't, regardless of qualifications. Once presented with a number, most people struggle to move far from it, even when they know, intellectually, that the figure was essentially arbitrary.

Realising how powerful anchors can be, some negotiation coaches now teach candidates to prepare a counteroffer in advance, rather than reacting to the anchor in the moment. Trained to recognise the bias, an experienced negotiator can consciously discount the anchor and argue instead from independent salary data. Having rehearsed their position beforehand, a candidate is far less likely to undervalue their own worth simply because the other side spoke first.
