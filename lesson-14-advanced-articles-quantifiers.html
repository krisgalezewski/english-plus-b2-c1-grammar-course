Advanced Articles & Quantifiers

Generic articles for generalisations, and formal quantifiers like "a number of" and "the majority of".

1 Warm-up

Articles (a/the/zero) can describe whole categories, not just individual things — "a manager should listen" or "the smartphone changed everything" are generalisations. Formal quantifiers like "a number of", "a great deal of" and "the majority of" add precision to those generalisations.

1. "The computer has transformed the modern office" uses "the" to talk about…

2. "Few employees complained" (with plain "few") suggests…

3. "A few employees complained" suggests…

💡 "Few/little" (no article) = almost none, a negative feel. "A few/a little" = some, a positive or neutral feel. The difference is entirely in that one small word "a".

3 The concept

Talking about categories, and measuring amounts precisely

Generic articles let us make broad statements about a whole class of things. Formal quantifiers add precision to how much or how many, and are especially common in reports, surveys and academic writing.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Generic reference: the + singular (inventions/species), a/an + singular (any typical example), or zero article + plural/uncountable (general category).
✅ Formal quantifiers: a number of / a great deal of + noun (a fair amount, formal), the majority of + noun (most of a group).
🎯 The tricky part: "few/little" and "a few/a little" look almost identical but carry opposite emotional colouring.

✅ "Few managers understood the new system." — negative: almost none did, implying a problem.
✅ "A few managers understood the new system." — neutral/positive: some did, framed as a reasonable number.
❌ Mixing them up completely reverses your meaning, even though the words look almost the same — always check for that tiny "a".

4 Reading — Survivorship Bias in Success Stories

The startup founder who drops out of university and builds a billion-dollar company has become one of the most repeated stories in modern business writing. Successful entrepreneurs are held up constantly as proof that talent and persistence are all it takes. What almost never gets mentioned is a number of equally talented, equally hardworking founders who tried the exact same approach and simply failed.

This distortion is known as survivorship bias: a great deal of our understanding of success comes exclusively from people who made it, while the majority of people who tried and failed simply disappear from the record. Failed ventures leave behind no bestselling memoir, no keynote speech, no inspiring interview — few of them are ever written about at all, because there is no obvious audience for a story that ends in quiet failure rather than triumphant success.

A few researchers have tried to correct for this by studying failed companies directly rather than relying on survivors' accounts, and their findings are sobering: identical habits, routines and even personality traits show up just as often among people who failed as among those who succeeded. In other words, perseverance and confidence may be far less predictive of success than the survivors themselves like to believe — luck and timing, it turns out, play a much larger role than most success stories are willing to acknowledge.
